Materialized abstraction

Classroom Materials

Montessori materials are not teaching aids in the ordinary sense. They are keys to the world — carefully designed objects that give abstract ideas a body the child can hold, explore and, through work, internalize.

Characteristics of quality materials

Before any characteristic comes the experimental criterion that generated all the others: "only the material that really interests a child, that she freely chooses and uses with regularity, is suited to her education" (The Discovery of the Child, ch. 6, p. 129). On that base, bought or handmade, a material that earns its shelf meets the same standards:

  • Isolation of the quality — each material makes one quality perceptible, through a graded series: "the objects are identical among themselves, except for the variable quality they possess" (ch. 6, p. 130).
  • Control of error — "every effort must be made so that the materials offered to the child contain within themselves a control of error" (p. 131); and it is not only the material's attribute: "Everything in the room, from the furniture to the children's special material, is an informant" (p. 132).
  • Aesthetics — "color, brightness and proportion are sought in everything that surrounds the child" (p. 132): beauty that invites use, not ornament that distracts.
  • Activity — the power to attract "does not depend so much on the quality of the thing itself as on the opportunity it gives the child to act" (p. 133). A material that is only looked at is not a Montessori material.
  • Limited in quantity — "it is a common but false belief that the child who has the most toys, the most help, would also be the most developed" (p. 134). The smelling jars exist to awaken the sense, not to teach odors: "a few jars are enough. An enormous quantity would not only confuse: it would be excess" (Montessori Jr., Education for Human Development, ch. 5, pp. 74–76).
  • Complete, real and in good repair — "each activity is complete; everything needed is present and in good condition […] the children use real sinks and refrigerators, not toy ones" (Seldin, Epstein & Howe, The Montessori Way, ch. 8, p. 70).
  • Coherence of the whole — the experimental study reported by Lillard strongly supports classrooms "in which the children use exclusively Montessori materials" (Lillard, ch. 11, p. 517); the legitimate path of variation is the extensions of the material itself (pp. 96–97).

Bought or handmade?

Both have their place — and their own criteria. In favor of the material bought from a serious maker, the argument is exactness and finish — especially where exactness is the content, as in the pink tower, whose pieces "differ by exactly one centimeter in each of the three dimensions […] a gift to the mathematical mind, which spontaneously perceives the exact degree of the difference" (Helfrich, NAMTA Journal 35/2, p. 91).

Handmade materials may seem cheaper, if time does not enter as a cost factor. The professional apparatus is not only more exact and less costly to obtain: it brings the beauty and professional finish we prize in the other parts of the environment. […] We could do long division with old shirt buttons. Instead we use the beautiful, though expensive, bead and tube material.
Gebhardt-Seele, Peter. NAMTA Journal 13/2 (p. 71).

The handmade material, in turn, is part of the elementary teacher's craft — much of the curriculum (cards, nomenclatures, timelines, command cards) was never produced industrially. David Kahn proposed treating it as institutional policy: the teacher "is paid to develop the classroom […] she will enrich the environment with her own research and her skill in making materials" — but "not at the expense of the class's functioning […] materials must not be made merely for the sake of accumulating" (Kahn, NAMTA Journal 1/1, pp. 34–36). The quality bar for the handmade is the same as for the bought, plus what only it has:

Are the handmade materials beautiful, clean, attractive, irresistible? […] it is our personal touches on these items that call the child and represent a manifestation of our love.
Bettmann, Joen. "True Work". NAMTA Journal 28/2 (pp. 75–76).

Two warnings close the theme. The first, from Lakshmi Kripalani, against accumulation: in her training the material was born in the classroom, with the children and the parents, and "everything was concise and full of purpose" (Kripalani, Montessori in Practice, pp. 165–166). The second, contemporary, against social-media aesthetics: Montessori Life warns that DIY blogs and fashionable handcrafted materials "are a sea of inspiration for photogenic shelves" — and asks whether they meet the criteria of Practical Life (Montessori Life, summer 2022, p. 36). Pretty in the feed is not a criterion; chosen, repeated and formative is.

Materialized abstraction

"Materialized abstraction" is the name Montessori gave to the design principle of her materials. Abstract ideas "do not exist by themselves, but in real things": weight does not exist on its own — it exists in heavy objects. To materialize is to give the quality a palpable, isolated body; the material presents the class, not the specimen — "they represent a quantity of leaves, not a single leaf" (The 1946 London Lectures, lecture 27, p. 254). And it does not teach facts: "it makes it possible for the children to reorganize their knowledge according to new principles. This increases their capacity to learn" (Montessori Jr., ch. 2, p. 25).

If we can materialize an abstract idea, present it in a form suited to the child — that is, under the appearance of palpable objects — will her mind be able to appreciate it, and take a deep interest in it?
Montessori, Maria. The Discovery of the Child (ch. 13, p. 232).

Observation answered yes. In mathematics the principle becomes a system: "we think of our sensorial material as a system of materialized abstractions, or of basic mathematics" (The Absorbent Mind, ch. 17, p. 210). The decimal system becomes golden beads the child can carry and exchange; the algebraic binomial becomes a cube that can be taken apart and rebuilt; grammar becomes symbols placed over words. The child manipulates the idea itself — and when abstraction finally arrives, it is not memorized: it is conquered, because the hands built it first.

The hands are the instruments of man's intelligence.
Montessori, Maria. The Absorbent Mind.

For the elementary years, the decisive point is the material's destiny: it is a bridge, not a dwelling. The classic image is the airplane:

The mind, in order to fly, needs to start from a point of contact, just as the airplane starts from its hangar. […] The child who, without inner impulse, is artificially "lifted aloft" by the teacher, who forces her to the abstract, can at most learn to descend slowly, like a parachute. She will never learn to "rise energetically to dizzying heights".
Montessori, Maria. The Advanced Montessori Method, vol. II (part IV, ch. 1, pp. 353–355).

Detachment comes from within, at each child's own time: Montessori records "the detachment the child shows, at a certain point, from the supports of arithmetic calculation […] as if obeying an inner impulse that seeks to free the soul from every material tie" (vol. I, ch. 3, pp. 96–97). The environment keeps the material available — without binding the child to it.

The child's work: didactic material × presentation

This distinction is the backbone of the method. A material is didactic when it teaches without a teacher; the presentation is the gesture — brief and calculated — by which the teacher connects the child to the material and withdraws.

The profound difference between our method and the so-called "object lessons" of the old systems is that the objects are not a help for the teacher. The objects, in our system, are a help for the child herself.
Montessori, Maria. The Discovery of the Child (ch. 10, p. 198).

What makes a material didactic — able to sustain the child's work on its own:

  • The control of error is in it. "The control of error is in the material itself, and the child has concrete evidence of it. […] It is at this point that the process of self-education begins" (Dr. Montessori's Own Handbook, p. 30).
  • The aim is inner, not performance. "The aim is not external — it is not that the child learn to place the cylinders. […] The aim is inner: that the child train herself to observe" (p. 31).
  • It calls for activity and repetition — the isolated quality, the exact series and the possibility of acting bring the child back to the material until conquest.
  • It makes the adult unnecessary: "while the child teaches herself and the material contains its own control of error, nothing remains for the teacher but to observe" (The Discovery of the Child, ch. 11, p. 214).

What the presentation is — the teacher's work, and its limits:

  • A call to attention, not a lecture (ch. 7, p. 135), governed by three characteristics: brevity ("the fewer the words, the more perfect the lesson"), simplicity ("nothing in it that is not absolute truth") and objectivity ("the teacher forgets herself and concentrates the child's attention entirely on the object") (p. 136).
  • A minimal, consented intrusion on freedom. "We admit that every lesson infringes on the child's freedom, and for this reason we allow it to last only a few seconds" (The Advanced Method, vol. I, ch. 2, pp. 59–60).
  • It shows the use and links child to object. For that, the teacher must know the material with her own hands: "she must exercise herself with it for a long time" (The Discovery of the Child, ch. 10, p. 200).
  • It does not correct. If the lesson was not understood, the teacher "must not insist by repeating it, and must abstain from making the child feel she has made a mistake" (ch. 7, p. 136) — only misuse of the material calls for interruption.
  • The right moment matters: "if the material is given too early, it is too difficult; too late, it bores; at the right time, it is something the child can conquer" (Montessori Jr., ch. 2, p. 28).

Laurie Ewert-Krocker organizes the two sides into a three-stage cycle — presentation → the child's work → observation — and locates the learning precisely:

In traditional education, the focus of learning is on the first and third stages — the adult's presentation and the assessment of what the child has mastered. […] Montessori education concentrates on the quality of the initial presentation and, above all, on the second period — the child's learning process, the opportunity for exploration, association, discovery and internalization.
Ewert-Krocker, Laurie. NAMTA Journal 36/1 (pp. 156–175).

The third stage is observation, not demand — sow as many seeds as possible, "but do not go digging up the seeds to see whether they are germinating" (M. Stephenson, qtd. in Ewert-Krocker, NAMTA 36/1). And Kripalani's final counterweight stands: material, tables and shelves are essential, "but mechanical. It is the teacher's inner preparation and her disposition that make the difference" (Kripalani, pp. 96–97).
