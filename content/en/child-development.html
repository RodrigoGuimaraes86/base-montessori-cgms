Theory and practice

Child Development

Montessori did not develop her ideas in isolation. Placing her work beside other great theorists of child development — and beside the reality of children who learn differently — sharpens our understanding of what the method is for.

Influential theorists

Maria Montessori was a contemporary of the pioneers who shaped modern developmental psychology. Comparing her method with two of them — Jean Piaget and Lev Vygotsky — shows both how much they share and where Montessori's approach is distinctive.

Jean Piaget (1896–1980)

Piaget, a Swiss psychologist, described intelligence as something the child constructs through interaction with the environment. His theory of cognitive development moves through four stages — sensorimotor, preoperational, concrete operational and formal operational — as the child builds and reorganizes mental structures (schemas) through assimilation and accommodation.

In common with Montessori

Both see the child as the active builder of their own intelligence; both grounded their theories in the patient observation of children; and both describe development as a sequence of qualitatively different stages — Piaget's stages echoing Montessori's planes.

Where they differ

Piaget was chiefly a researcher describing how children think; Montessori was an educator designing an environment and materials to support them. Montessori also found that, with concrete materials, children often reach operational understanding earlier than Piaget's typical ages suggest.

Practical application: offer concrete, manipulable materials before abstraction; match each material to the child's developmental readiness; and let the child build concepts through their own repeated activity rather than through the adult's explanation.

Lev Vygotsky (1896–1934)

Vygotsky, a Russian psychologist, emphasized that learning is fundamentally social and cultural. His best-known idea is the Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) — the distance between what a child can do alone and what they can do with the guidance of a "more knowledgeable other" — supported by scaffolding and by language as a tool of thought.

In common with Montessori

Both value the social and cultural dimension of learning. The Montessori multi-age classroom is almost a picture of the ZPD: older children scaffold younger ones, and language and community are central to development.

Where they differ

Vygotsky places the adult or peer at the center as mediator. Montessori places the prepared environment and the child's self-directed work at the center, with the adult stepping back — the "scaffold" is often a self-correcting material or a peer, not the teacher's instruction.

Practical application: use mixed-age grouping and peer teaching; give grace and courtesy lessons that build the social community; and design materials that scaffold the child toward independence.

Montessori also stood alongside John Dewey (learning by doing) and, later, Erik Erikson (psychosocial stages): like them, she insisted that education must meet the child's developmental needs through meaningful activity, not through passive reception.

The child is the builder of man… It is the child who makes the man, and no man exists who was not made by the child he once was.
Montessori, Maria. The Absorbent Mind.

Learning exceptionalities

Montessori's method did not begin with "typical" children. It began, historically, with children who learned differently — through her study of the French physicians Jean Itard and Édouard Séguin and her work with children with disabilities. That origin makes the method naturally attentive to individual differences, and much of what helps one child helps all.

Types found in elementary children

Among the exceptionalities a guide may meet in the 6–12 classroom:

  • Specific learning disabilities — including dyslexia (reading), dyscalculia (mathematics) and dysgraphia (writing).
  • Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) — differences in attention, impulse control and activity level.
  • Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) — differences in social communication and sensory processing, with focused interests.
  • Sensory processing differences — over- or under-sensitivity to sensory input.
  • Speech and language differences — in articulation, fluency or comprehension.
  • Intellectual disability — differences in reasoning and adaptive skills.
  • Giftedness and twice-exceptional (2e) — advanced ability, sometimes alongside a co-occurring disability.

How the Montessori environment helps

  • Multisensory materials engage sight, touch and movement — a support for many learners.
  • Control of error lets the child correct themselves, protecting confidence.
  • Individual pace and long work cycles remove time pressure.
  • Freedom of movement serves children who need to move to focus.
  • Mixed-age community allows a child to work at their level without stigma.
  • Concrete-to-abstract sequence gives every learner a path into abstraction.

Glossary

IEP (Individualized Education Program)
A written plan of goals, services and accommodations for a student who qualifies for special education.
504 Plan
A plan of accommodations that gives a student with a disability equal access to learning, without changing what is taught.
Accommodation
A change in how a child learns or is assessed (e.g., extra time), without changing the content or expectation.
Modification
A change in what a child is expected to learn (e.g., different or reduced content).
Differentiation
Adjusting teaching to meet the varied readiness, interests and needs of learners.
Inclusion
Educating children with and without disabilities together, with the supports each one needs.
Dyslexia · Dyscalculia · Dysgraphia
Specific learning differences affecting, respectively, reading, mathematics and writing.
ADHD
Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder: differences in attention, impulse control and activity level.
ASD (Autism Spectrum Disorder)
A developmental difference in social communication and sensory processing, with a wide range of expression.
Sensory Processing Disorder (SPD)
Difficulty organizing and responding to sensory information from the body and environment.
Twice-exceptional (2e)
A child who is gifted and has a co-occurring disability or learning difference.
Executive function
The mental skills for planning, focusing attention, remembering instructions and managing tasks.
Scaffolding
Temporary support that helps a child do what they cannot yet do alone, gradually withdrawn as they gain independence.
Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD)
Vygotsky's term for the gap between what a child can do alone and what they can do with guidance.
