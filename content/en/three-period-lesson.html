The Three Period Lesson

The three-period lesson is one of the most important techniques in the Montessori Method. The teacher must be attentive to use this teaching and assessment approach at the right moment, in a way that supports the child's development.

As the name suggests, the technique is divided into three periods:

1
Definition of the concept. The adult defines the concept clearly and verbally, illustrating what is being shown. For example: "This is red." / "This is blue." It is possibly the first time the child meets that idea or vocabulary, and it may need to be reinforced several times.
2
Association of the concept. The adult engages the child in playful tasks that help them associate the concept. For example: "Can you point to the red?" / "Can you pick the blue?" This is the most important period — when the concept is consolidated — and deserves the most attention from both child and adult.
3
Confirmation of the concept. A gentle form of assessment to confirm whether the child has understood. For example: "What color is this?" / "What is this?" Depending on the answer, it may be necessary to return to a previous period or move on to the next lesson.

The three-period lesson at each plane and age

Children aged 0 to 3

For these very young children, typically only the first two periods are used, avoiding the questions and assessment of the third period. We do not want children to feel pressured or to make mistakes, so this third stage is left out.

Children aged 3 to 6

All three periods are used — but never one right after another within a single lesson. It is essential to spend ample time in the second period, so that the child internalizes the concept and the third period yields the expected response.

Children in the Second Plane (6 to 12)

The same fundamental idea applies, but the application expands:

  • First period — the Key Lesson. The concept is introduced with much more scope than for younger children: often through a story, rich in content and designed to capture interest and make the child want to learn more. For example, an introduction to photosynthesis can be told as a story about how the process works, introducing the relevant terms.
  • Second period — exploration. The child explores the concept through projects, research, activities and exercises, alone or in groups, expanding and absorbing it. Continuing the example, children might research different plants and how they photosynthesize, then go outside to examine specimens, including under a microscope.
  • Third period — confirmation. This can take many forms: a written test, a construction project, an oral, theatrical or artistic presentation — as well as the adult observing the child's work. The children might, for instance, present to the whole class everything they researched.

Observation makes it all work
