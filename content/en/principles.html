Guiding Principles

Montessori wrote extensively about her method and philosophy. Among all that material, some aspects can be called the fundamentals. These were systematized by the Center for Guided Montessori Studies (CGMS) team — John Wolff, Tim Seldin, Kitty Bravo and Marc Seldin — into thirteen guiding principles.

1
Encourage independence. Provide an environment that allows each child the freedom to direct their own life and explore their own path to empowerment. The focus shifts from the adult to the child; the adult's role is to prepare an environment that supports free, responsible choice — where children also learn to care for their surroundings and for others.
2
Support the natural learning process. Focus on each child's developmental needs and interests, using their sensitive periods as your guide. Montessori taught that every child has an inner power — horme — that guides their learning; we must trust that power, which differs at each plane of development.
3
Create a diverse, multi-age community. Mixed-age groups bring richer levels of understanding, let children learn from one another, and give each child three years to develop each area at their own pace. Everyone gets to be youngest, middle and eldest — favoring cooperation over competition.
4
Cultivate concentration. Concentration is where transformation occurs, so we protect it: never interrupt a concentrating child (as long as no living being is harmed and the environment is respected). Grace and courtesy lessons help build the calm, orderly atmosphere concentration needs.
5
Make learning a springboard. Turn learning into spontaneous exploration and discovery, creative self-expression and community service — caring for the environment and everyone in it — progressing not only intellectually but also in respect and empathy.
6
Foster broad human capability. Nurture spiritual awareness, ethical decision-making, emotional intelligence, executive function, social responsibility and a healthy lifestyle. The adult must model all of this, and the child must be given the chance to practice and to be accountable for their actions.
7
Provide freedom of movement and engage the senses. The classroom is designed for movement — humans need it to develop. The guide provides constant opportunities for children to move and learn, and sensory education becomes the foundation for observation and imagination.
8
Foster cooperation, not competition. We want a cooperative community in which each child sees the others as people to count on. Competition should never be the motivator for learning; the only healthy competition is one where all participants grow and develop together.
9
Balance freedom within limits. Understand clearly what the limits are and why they exist — that is how children come to see that they have freedom, but also boundaries that guide them toward full growth. When children make mistakes, we respectfully show them the consequences so they can choose better next time.
10
Nurture peacemakers. Help each child become a peaceful individual and a peacemaker in the classroom, local and global communities. The educator models how to resolve conflict, negotiate, develop empathy and understand one's own emotions.
11
Be a living example of normalization. Use daily reflective practices to gain self-awareness of what you bring to the classroom and to prepare yourself spiritually for each child. Children follow our example, so we must model personal development and self-care.
12
Trust the child. Trust the child to reveal their true nature and needs through meaningful work. Observe and honor each tiny, delicate manifestation of the opening life in the child's soul — given the right environment and guidance, the child will blossom.
13
Present with grace, order and patience. Introduce the prepared environment, its materials and its lessons with grace, order and patience — because that is how the child will do it in turn. After presenting, step back and observe the soul self-constructing through meaningful work.

In Montessori's own words

The main thing is that the groups should contain different ages, because it has a great influence on the cultural development of the child… You cannot imagine how well a young child learns from an older child; how patient the older child is with the difficulties of the younger.
Montessori, Maria & Yesson, Heather. The Child, Society and the World (p. 70).

The great principle which brings success to the teacher is this: as soon as concentration has begun, act as if the child does not exist.
Montessori, Maria. The Absorbent Mind (p. 272).

Young people must have enough freedom to allow them to act on individual initiative. But in order that individual action should be free and useful at the same time, it must be restricted within certain limits and rules that give the necessary guidance.
Montessori, Maria. From Childhood to Adolescence (p. 85).
