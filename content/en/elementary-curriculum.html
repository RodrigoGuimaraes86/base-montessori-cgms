Cosmic Education

The Elementary Curriculum

The Montessori elementary school is not the Children's House with harder content — it is the answer to another child, who at six changes personality. Cosmic curriculum, spiral curriculum and "follow the child": three ideas that form a single pedagogical design.

Another child, another school

At six, the intelligence turns outward and begins to demand the reasons of things; the mind is "a fertile field", ready to receive the seeds of all culture — and if the sowing is delayed, the interest will no longer be there (To Educate the Human Potential, ch. 1, p. 10).

  • The moral field. The child wants to distinguish good from evil by their own judgment and resents arbitrary authority — which is why the elementary years work with justice, discussed rules and the history of humanity as moral history (ch. 1, p. 11).
  • An organized social instinct. The child seeks the group "in some form of organized activity", with a leader and roles — hence the small groups, the collective projects and going out (ch. 1, p. 11).
  • The frame is the five Great Lessons, told every year: the Story of the Universe, the Coming of Life, the Coming of Human Beings, the Story of Language and the Story of Mathematics — impressionistic oral narratives from which every area branches (Dorer, Grammar Tells a Story, pp. 49–51).
  • From story to study. The pattern is "story and study": the teacher's impressionistic story, then the children's autonomous research (Duffy & Duffy, Children of the Universe, ch. 1).
  • A teacher with no program to hide behind. "He himself must acquire a reasonable knowledge of all the subjects", without sheltering "behind program and timetable" (To Educate the Human Potential, ch. 1, pp. 12–13).

Education between the ages of six and twelve is not a direct continuation of that which has gone before, though it is built upon that basis. Psychologically, there is a decisive change of personality.
Montessori, Maria. To Educate the Human Potential (ch. 1, p. 10).

The Cosmic Curriculum

How do you feed a mind that wants the reasons for everything without fragmenting it into subjects? By giving the whole before the parts. Through the Five Great Lessons the child receives a grand narrative into which every later study fits: geography, biology, history, language and mathematics all become chapters of the same story.

  • The universe as organizing center. The vision of the whole fixes the child's mind and makes interest spread to everything, "for all things are linked and have their place in the universe"; one cannot understand "a stone without some understanding of the great sun" (To Educate the Human Potential, ch. 1, pp. 13–14).
  • Admiration before interest. Well presented, the idea of the universe creates "admiration and wonder, a feeling loftier than any interest" (ch. 1, p. 13).
  • The cosmic task. Each being has, beyond the conscious purpose of living, an unconscious function in service of the whole — the "drama of the ocean": corals and protozoa as "volunteers" purifying the seas. The story flows into the child's questions: "What am I? What is the task of man in this wonderful universe?" (ch. 5, pp. 32–37; ch. 1, p. 14).
  • Sowing all the sciences. Give of each branch an impression, not the exact detail: at 6–12 there is "a kind of sensitive period for the imagination" (From Childhood to Adolescence, ch. 5).
  • The demand on the adult. "It is not enough for the teacher to limit herself to loving and understanding the child: she must first love and understand the universe" (From Childhood to Adolescence, ch. 5).
  • Origin and reach. The plan was set out in England in 1935 and matured in the Indian period (Kodaikanal); for the Duffys, the elementary years are the "sensitive period" of cosmic education; for Stephenson, the cosmic vision runs through all the planes (Duffy & Duffy, ch. 1; Stephenson, Montessori Cosmic Education, pp. 6–7).

Since it has been seen to be necessary to give so much to the child, let us give him a vision of the whole universe. The universe is an imposing reality, and an answer to all questions. […] all things are part of the universe, and are connected with each other to form one whole unity.
Montessori, Maria. To Educate the Human Potential (ch. 1, p. 13).

The Spiral Curriculum

The term belongs to Jerome Bruner (1960); the practice — the great themes returning each cycle with more depth and abstraction — was already at work in Montessori schools half a century earlier. Nothing is "finished" in one term: multiplication begins in the senses with the bead bars, returns in the memorization work, reappears in the checkerboard and the bank game, and finally dissolves into pure abstraction.

  • The seed that germinates later. On the chemistry of water: "We cannot yet give him great theories […] But at this age he must receive the seeds, which will germinate later" (From Childhood to Adolescence, ch. 5).
  • From concrete to abstract. Each material materializes a concept and is gradually left behind as the abstraction consolidates; the great themes "return every three years with greater depth and abstraction" (Seldin, Epstein & Howe, The Montessori Way, ch. 8).
  • Indirect preparation. Each work secretly prepares the next: the cylinders prepare the pencil grip, the golden beads prepare the decimal system — and the Great Lessons meet, at every return, a child able to draw more from them.
  • Bruner ratifies Montessori. Annette Haines shows Bruner's spiral curriculum describing exactly what the Montessori materials were already doing (Haines, NAMTA Journal 25/1, 2000).
  • The spiral tracks the brain. Revisiting and deepening between 7 and 11 coincides with brain growth in the language areas and parietal cortex (Haines, NAMTA Journal 31/2, 2006).
  • The spiral's limit. It is a figure of the 6–12 years: for the adolescent, the third plane calls for another organization (NAMTA Journal 26/3, 2001).

Education between the ages of six and twelve is not a direct continuation of that which has gone before, though it is built upon that basis.
Montessori, Maria. From Childhood to Adolescence.

It is in childhood that the first interest must be awakened, the seed of all the sciences must be sown.
Montessori, Maria. From Childhood to Adolescence (appendices).

"Follow the Child"

The most repeated — and most misunderstood — phrase in the Montessori vocabulary. The curriculum is a map, not a schedule: it shows every road, but the moment of each journey is decided by observation. A child fascinated by volcanoes will be led — through that fascination — into geology, chemistry, geography and history. Interest is not a distraction from the curriculum; it is the entrance to it.

  • It is the general rule, not an episode. "The need to follow the child is clearly demonstrated here, but it is in fact the rule for every side of education and in every field" (Education for a New World, ch. 9, p. 64).
  • Following costs the adult. They must suspend their own mission to follow the child, "adapting to her rhythm and to the psychological needs of her growth" (The Secret of Childhood, p. 198).
  • In the elementary years, to follow is to respond, never to dictate. "Our teaching must only answer the mental needs of the child, never dictate them"; and the engine is internal — "only duty and fatigue can be induced from outside, never interest!" (To Educate the Human Potential, ch. 1, pp. 12–13).
  • Following is not permitting everything. "It does not mean that everything the child does with the educational materials is valid": the pink tower is not building blocks; the teacher gently interrupts misuse and investigates the real need behind the choice (Stephenson, Please Help Me Do It Myself, pp. 14, 25).
  • The instrument is observation. The record of concentration, choices and repetitions says which lesson to offer next — in the elementary years, with the student progressively taking over their own record (Stephenson).
  • Closing the circle. The cosmic curriculum puts everything within reach precisely so there is something to follow; the spiral guarantees that, wherever interest leads, a prepared path returns to the theme at a higher level.

To really help him, the adult must follow the child, and not expect the child to follow him. […] The child has his own laws of growth, and if we want to help him grow, we must follow him instead of imposing ourselves upon him.
Montessori, Maria. Education for a New World (ch. 9, p. 64).

Following the child does not mean the absence of structure. The structure is all there — in the prepared environment, in the records, in the sequence of the materials. What observation decides is the when and the through which door. That is how freedom and curriculum, so often seen as opposites, become the same movement.
