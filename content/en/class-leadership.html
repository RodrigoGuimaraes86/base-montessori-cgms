Leading the community

Class Leadership

Leading a Montessori class is not about commanding from the front of the room. It is about building — patiently and deliberately — a community in which freedom and responsibility grow together, guided by observation and respect.

Planning and record keeping

In a Montessori class, planning does not mean writing one lesson for the whole group. The guide plans for each child, based on what observation reveals: which presentations were given, which were repeated, which materials the child has mastered, and what should come next. Careful records are what make freedom possible — because the guide always knows where each child is on their path, the child can be free to choose without ever being lost.

Records also protect the work cycle. When we know what has been presented and what the child is working on, we do not need to interrupt to check progress; we observe, take notes, and plan the next step for the right moment.

Grace and courtesy

Grace and courtesy lessons are short, often playful presentations that model the social life of the community: how to greet someone, how to interrupt politely, how to offer help — and how to refuse it kindly —, how to walk around a rug, how to wait for a material that is in use. In the second plane, they grow into conversations about fairness, honesty and how we treat one another, matching the child's sensitive period for justice and moral judgment.

These lessons are the foundation of the calm and orderly atmosphere the class needs. We do not demand respectful behavior; we present it, model it, and let the community practice it until it belongs to everyone.

Community building

The multi-age classroom is a small society. Children care for the environment, for the plants and animals, organize celebrations, hold class meetings and solve problems together. For the elementary child — in full social expansion — the community itself is the engine of development: it is where leadership is practiced, agreements are negotiated and belonging is built.

Informed guidance and positive discipline

Discipline in a Montessori class is not imposed from outside; it emerges from within, through meaningful work and clear limits. The guide intervenes respectfully — redirecting energy toward purposeful activity, showing the consequences of actions and helping the child choose better next time. An informed guide acts from observation, not from reaction.

Discipline must come through liberty. […] We do not consider an individual disciplined only when he has been rendered as artificially silent as a mute and as immovable as a paralytic. He is an individual annihilated, not disciplined.
Montessori, Maria. The Montessori Method (p. 86).

The first idea that the child must acquire, in order to be actively disciplined, is that of the difference between good and evil; and the task of the educator lies in seeing that the child does not confound good with immobility, and evil with activity.
Montessori, Maria. The Montessori Method (p. 86).

Rights and responsibilities

Every member of the community — child and adult — has rights: to work without being disturbed, to choose their activity, to be treated with respect. And every right carries a responsibility: to care for the materials, to respect the work of others, to contribute to the life of the group. When rights and responsibilities are made explicit — ideally built with the children — rules stop being impositions and become shared agreements that the community itself protects.
