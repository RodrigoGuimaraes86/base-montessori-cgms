Biography

Maria Montessori

Scientist · Reformer · Feminist · Peacemaker · Visionary. Maria Montessori (1870–1952) was a physician, educator and philosopher, internationally recognized for creating the method that bears her name.

Maria Montessori was born in Chiaravalle, Italy, and was one of the first women to graduate in Medicine from the University of Rome, in 1896. Her scientific training and her interest in social issues were fundamental to the development of her pedagogical ideas.

From medicine to education

Montessori began her professional career as a doctor, working with children with mental disabilities — an experience that sparked her interest in education. She observed that many of these children were not being treated appropriately, and that they could learn much more effectively if given the right environment and the correct educational approach. This experience led her to rethink traditional educational methods and to study the work of Jean Itard and Édouard Séguin.

This way of observing children led her to develop the method scientifically: always forming a hypothesis, putting it into practice, and then observing the children. Her entire process consistently considered the child in a free environment, with activities that sparked genuine interest.

The first Casa dei Bambini

Her journey in education officially began in 1907, when she was invited to work with children in a poor neighborhood of Rome, where she founded the first Casa dei Bambini (Children's House). There, through the active observation of children's lives, she began to develop the foundations of what would become her Method.

Maria Montessori believed that no human being is educated by another person, but rather by themselves.

The core ideas of the method

  • Respect for the child: the child is an active participant in learning, capable of making choices and learning at their own pace — with a natural inner guide that leads their development.
  • Prepared environment: the classroom is organized and designed so the child can explore freely, with materials that promote hands-on learning and autonomy.
  • Self-directed learning: the child is free to choose their activities and explore the world around them, developing cognitive, social and motor skills.
  • The role of the educator: the teacher is a guide or facilitator, not a traditional instructor. The key word is observation — always active, always seeking to understand the child's needs. Every interaction is a pedagogical opportunity.

Education for all

The Montessori philosophy includes a belief in the importance of education for everyone. Montessori was an advocate of inclusive education: she believed that all children, regardless of their background or abilities, have the potential to learn and develop meaningfully. These ideas ran completely contrary to the prevailing notions of the time and had a profound impact on how child development is understood.

A feminist and a peacemaker

Montessori was also an advocate of the idea that all women should be educated and empowered, as this was crucial for the development of society as a whole. She was engaged in feminist activity during her medical studies and consistently defended the poor women who suffered under the male-dominated Italian government of the time. She wrote:

[A woman] works like a man, and her domestic responsibilities do not cease to exist. Instead of the rest that her husband has after work, household tasks await her, usually with a child at her heart or in her arms.
(apud Povell, pp. 37–8)

She was a contemporary of the New Education movement of the late 19th and early 20th centuries, alongside figures such as John Dewey, Ovide Decroly, Célestin Freinet and Adolphe Ferrière. Seeking a child-focused development aimed at a peaceful society, Montessori eventually distanced herself from the movement due to a lack of alignment with its ideas.

Averting war is the work of politicians; establishing peace is the work of education.
Montessori, Maria. Education and Peace (p. 24).

Legacy

Montessori spent the last years of her life traveling the world, promoting her ideas and training educators, while her schools and methods spread across many countries. She wrote several books and gave conferences on her discoveries and theories.

Maria Montessori passed away in 1952, but her legacy lives on. The Montessori Method remains one of the most popular and effective educational approaches, adopted by schools worldwide and helping millions of children become independent, confident learners. Her work stays relevant because it places the child at the center of the educational process — respecting their needs and promoting integral development, not only academically, but also emotionally and socially.

    Born in Chiaravalle, Italy.
