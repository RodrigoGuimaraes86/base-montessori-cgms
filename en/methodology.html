In practice

The Methodology

How Montessori principles become a concrete routine: a prepared environment, materials organized by area, freedom of choice, long work periods and an adult who observes and guides.

The prepared environment and its areas

The Montessori classroom is organized into areas of knowledge, each with materials arranged in increasing order of complexity and within the child's reach. The child moves freely among them and chooses their own work.

1
Practical life — everyday activities (pouring water, buttoning, sweeping, caring for plants) that develop coordination, independence, concentration and order.
2
Sensorial — materials that refine the senses and help the child classify and understand the world: dimension, color, shape, texture, sound, weight.
3
Language — from concrete to abstract: sandpaper letters, the movable alphabet, vocabulary enrichment, reading and writing.
4
Mathematics — concrete materials (rods, golden beads, spindles) that make quantity, the decimal system and operations tangible.
5
Culture and sciences — geography, history, biology, art and music, broadening the child's view of the world.

The work cycle

A striking feature is the long, uninterrupted work period (often around three hours). Without constant interruptions, the child can choose an activity, immerse themselves in it, repeat it as many times as needed, and reach deep concentration. To respect this cycle is to respect the child's inner rhythm.

The three-period lesson

To introduce new concepts and vocabulary, the guide often uses the three-period lesson:

  1. Naming — "This is…". The guide names the object or quality.
  2. Recognition — "Show me…". The child associates the name with the object.
  3. Recall — "What is this?". The child names it on their own.

Freedom of choice and the role of the guide

The child chooses what to do, for how long and with whom, within the limits of the environment and respect for the group. The adult observes continuously, presents the materials at the right moment, and intervenes as little as possible — their goal is to become, little by little, unnecessary.

“The greatest sign of success for a teacher is to be able to say: ‘The children are now working as if I did not exist.’”

Mixed-age groups

Classes usually bring together three age groups in the same space. This coexistence fosters cooperation, language, leadership and empathy — the older children teach, the younger ones look up to them, and everyone advances at their own pace.

From concrete to abstract

One logic runs through the entire method: the child first experiences with their hands — manipulating concrete materials — and only then internalizes the abstract concept. For Montessori, the hand is the instrument of intelligence.
