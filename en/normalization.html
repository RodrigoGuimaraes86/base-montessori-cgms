The child revealed

Normalization

Normalization is perhaps the most misunderstood word in the Montessori vocabulary. It does not mean making children "normal" by force. It names the moment a child's scattered energies fuse into one working personality — the child's own return to their true, undistorted nature, when the obstacles are removed and the conditions are right.

The central result

Montessori did not treat normalization as a welcome side effect: it was, in her words, the most important single result of her whole work — a phenomenon that repeats "unfailingly in all our schools, with children belonging to different social classes, races and civilizations" (The Absorbent Mind, ch. 19, p. 230).

  • Deviations, not personality. Disorder, aimless movement, fantasy detached from reality, possessiveness — these "come from a failure to organize the personality", when the child's hand, mind and will develop apart from one another (ch. 19, p. 229).
  • "Good" children are deviated too. Society sorts children into the naughty, the good (passive) and the superior (exuberant); all three sets of traits vanish with real work — so all three were masks (ch. 19, p. 227; The 1946 London Lectures, lecture 31, p. 282).
  • Illness, not immorality. "Naughtiness is an expression of deviant development" — an ill-functioning, not a sin; therefore "bad behavior cannot be corrected by direct intervention" (The 1946 London Lectures, lecture 30, p. 277).
  • A misleading word, openly acknowledged. The term "suggests that teachers help children who are not normal become normal. This is not what Dr. Montessori meant to suggest at all" (Seldin & Epstein, The Montessori Way, p. 55).

We get the unification of all the energies and the creation of a new person. We call this normalization. This phenomenon of normalization is only achieved through intense activity and real concentration.
Montessori, Maria. The 1946 London Lectures (lecture 30, p. 279).

How it happens

Normalization cannot be taught, preached or rewarded into existence. It has exactly one doorway — deep, freely chosen concentration on real work done with the hands — and it arrives suddenly, not gradually. The adult's contribution is entirely indirect: prepare motives for activity, then protect the concentration.

  • Work done by the hands with real things, "accompanied by mental concentration" — concentration, not mere occupation (The Absorbent Mind, ch. 19, pp. 230, 232–233).
  • Free choice among "motives for activity" matched to the child's interests, in a prepared environment; freedom as license only frees the deviations (pp. 231–232).
  • The change "appears all of a sudden" after deep concentration: "No sooner has he found his work than his defects disappear" (p. 228).

Normalization comes about through "concentration" on a piece of work. For this we must provide "motives for activity" so well adapted to the child's interests that they provoke his deep attention.
Montessori, Maria. The Absorbent Mind (ch. 19, p. 232).

An interesting piece of work, freely chosen, which has the virtue of inducing concentration rather than fatigue, adds to the child's energies and mental capacities, and leads him to self-mastery.
Montessori, Maria. The Absorbent Mind.

Once a child concentrates, all his naughtiness disappears, just as darkness disappears when a light is switched on.
Montessori, Maria. The 1946 London Lectures (lecture 31, p. 281).

What it looks like

E. M. Standing, Montessori's biographer and collaborator, left the classic list of the characteristics of the normalized child (Standing, qtd. in Seldin & Epstein, pp. 55–56):

1
A love of order — the cared-for, predictable environment as a need, not an imposition.
2
A love of work — the child seeks meaningful activity spontaneously and works with joy.
3
Profound spontaneous concentration — long, absorbed engagement with freely chosen work.
4
Attachment to reality — preferring real work and real experience to fantasy detached from the world.
5
Love of silence and of working alone — recollection as a choice, not isolation.
6
Sublimation of the possessive instinct — from "it's mine" to caring for what belongs to all.
7
Obedience — maturing together with the will, instead of being born of fear.
8
Joy — the outward sign of development going well.
9
The power to act from real choice — not from impulse or command, but from one's own decision.

Deviations disappear through work

Montessori observed that many behaviors we treat as character flaws — caprice, disorder, possessiveness, restlessness — are in fact deviations: masks the child wears when their development finds obstacles. The remarkable discovery of the Casa dei Bambini was that these masks fall away, not through correction or punishment, but through concentrated work. When the child meets an activity that truly engages their inner needs, the deviation simply becomes unnecessary.

This is why the guide's response to a disruptive child is not, first, to discipline — it is to observe, and to help that child find the work that will call forth their concentration. The work heals what the correction cannot reach.

A unique type of child appears, a "new child"; but really it is the child's true "personality" allowed to construct itself normally.
Montessori, Maria. The Absorbent Mind (ch. 19, p. 229).

Where the two transformations meet

The two themes — the adult's transformation and the child's — lock together like a hinge. Normalization only ever happens through the child's own concentrated work; and the child only reaches that work if an adult has prepared the environment, offered the right motives of activity, and had the discipline not to interrupt. Every one of those conditions is a fruit of the teacher's transformation.

  • The untransformed adult, armed with anger disguised as authority, breaks concentration with corrections, praise and help — and so keeps producing the very deviations they then punish.
  • The transformed teacher treats the child's first concentration as sacred, "acts as if the child does not exist", and reads any disorder in the class as her own error to find and fix (The Absorbent Mind, ch. 27, pp. 315, 320).
  • The direction of influence then reverses: the normalized children elevate the adult — "the child has made her grow till she is brought within his sphere" (ch. 27, p. 318).

Experience tells us that we must not consider the teacher and the child separately… The adult, the child, and the environment are a trinity. They have to be considered as one.
Montessori, Maria. The 1946 London Lectures (lecture 30, p. 274).
