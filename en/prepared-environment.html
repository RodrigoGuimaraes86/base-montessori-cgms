The silent teacher

The Prepared Environment

The prepared environment is not a school-decorating technique: it is the architectural consequence of a psychological thesis. If the child constructs themselves — if no one can learn for them — then the only way to educate is to act indirectly, preparing the setting in which that self-construction will happen.

What makes an environment "prepared"

In Montessori's work, the environment is one of the three factors that sustain the method — "the environment, the teacher and the material" (The Secret of Childhood, ch. 8, p. 153) — and its qualities were not invented at a desk: they were discovered, by watching what children chose and what they abandoned. The first environment at San Lorenzo was not even beautiful — tables "made to be strong and durable", an "enormous, massive" cupboard (ch. 7, p. 130). It was the children's concentrated activity that taught what should stay and what should go; years later, at the Messina orphanage, refinement was already the program: tiny plates, cutlery and napkins, "everything in proportion to the small hands" (ch. 8, p. 154).

The term "prepared environment" is a key term that arises from the premise that adults do not teach children: children teach themselves — as all human beings do — not by being instructed by others, but by interacting with the environment, following their human tendencies, and adapting themselves to the environment and the environment to themselves.
Ewert-Krocker, Laurie. "The Prepared Environment". NAMTA Journal 26/3 (pp. 452–453).

  • Order — each material has one place, and the sequence on the shelves follows the sequence of learning, from simple to complex.
  • Beauty and simplicity — a clean, attractive space with nothing superfluous, communicating respect for the child.
  • Accessibility — child-sized furniture and real, working tools within reach; the child does not need to ask permission to act.
  • Real, fragile objects — the dish that breaks and the glass that betrays the rough gesture are "the denouncers of rough, disordered, undisciplined movements" (The Advanced Method, vol. I, ch. 5, p. 157): control of error built into daily life.
  • Freedom of movement — room to walk, to choose a table or a rug, to work alone or in a group.
  • Limits within freedom — generally one of each material: children learn to wait, to share the environment and to respect the work of others.

The essential thing, above all, is that it be "artistically beautiful". Here beauty is not produced by the superfluous or by luxury, but by grace and harmony of line and color, joined to that absolute simplicity demanded by the lightness of the furniture. […] we must not seek richness of material, but refinement of spirit.
Montessori, Maria. The Advanced Montessori Method, vol. I (ch. 5, p. 155).

And the whole environment works as a moral educator. Its opposite — the "child-proof" environment — hides the error: the child "who drops a metal plate on the floor a hundred times without breaking it remains immersed in his sea of defects without noticing them" — an environment that conceals and therefore encourages errors, "with Mephistophelian hypocrisy" (vol. I, ch. 5, p. 158).

The first aim of the prepared environment is, as far as it is possible, to render the growing child independent of the adult.
Montessori, Maria. The Secret of Childhood.

The second-plane environment: the room plus the world

The decisive question of the elementary years is: what changes in the environment when the child changes? Montessori insists that from seven to twelve there is not mere growth but "true metamorphoses" (From Childhood to Adolescence, ch. 1). Three changes redesign the environment:

  • The child needs to go out. "From seven to twelve years, the child needs to enlarge his field of action. […] he needs wider boundaries for his social experiences" — and the closed school "can no longer be sufficient": the "anomalies" that appear "are simply reactions to an environment that has become inadequate" (ch. 2). That is why going out is not a field trip: it is the structural extension of the environment. "There is no description, no image in any book that is capable of replacing the sight of real trees […] in a real forest" (ch. 5).
  • The child passes to the abstract. "The passage to the second level of education is the passage from the sensorial, material level to the abstract" (ch. 2). The engine is no longer the senses but the imagination: "Imagination is the great power of this age. […] The instruction of children from seven to twelve years must appeal to the imagination" (ch. 5) — hence Cosmic Education as the curricular answer.
  • The moral sense and organized group life are born. "In the field of morality, the child now needs his own inner light" (To Educate the Human Potential, p. 11); he seeks the group "in some form of organized activity […] in which each has a different status" (p. 11). The limits change in kind: "Its limits are no longer the walls of a room, but only the checks of a moral order" (From Childhood to Adolescence, ch. 3).

From seven to twelve years, the child needs to enlarge his field of action. […] In the second period, the child needs wider boundaries for his social experiences.
Montessori, Maria. From Childhood to Adolescence (ch. 2).

The closed school, as conceived today, can no longer be sufficient for him. Something is missing for the full development of his personality. We notice a certain regression — manifestations of character we call anomalies; they are simply reactions to an environment that has become inadequate.
Montessori, Maria. From Childhood to Adolescence (ch. 2).

Kay Baker translates this into a design formula: fill the school with motives for activity, fill it with opportunities for choice, and fill it "with an expansion of the field of action, so that freedom to choose and act within the prepared environment leads to freedom to choose and act in the larger environment of society" (Baker, NAMTA Journal 26/1, p. 77). The expected outcome of adaptation, in this plane, is for the child to "feel at home on the planet" (p. 74) — and, for moral development, "the most important element of the environment is the Going Out program" (pp. 86–87).

Lower Elementary (6–9): the sowing

Tradition divides the second plane into two subphases — and the division is not administrative but psychological: in the first, "the child's main activity is the accumulation of the raw material needed to build the mind and personality"; in the second, "the analysis of the raw material accumulated in the first" (Gupta, NAMTA Journal 17/1). The 6–9 years are the time of sowing — the phase of the Great Lessons, of the curriculum's horizontal sweep, of many concepts presented as keys. And Montessori is maximalist about quantity: "If I am asked how many seeds may be sown, my answer is: 'As many as possible!'" (To Educate the Human Potential, p. 10).

During each period the child's mentality remains the same, but in the first subphase the child's main activity is the accumulation of the raw material needed to build the mind and personality. The main activity of the second subphase is the analysis of the raw material accumulated in the first.
Gupta, Rajendra. "Needs of the Elementary-Age Child". NAMTA Journal 17/1 (pp. 97–133).

  • An open, enthusiastic child — "the six-to-nine-year-olds are so sweet, so open-hearted. Everything you give them is received with joy and enthusiasm" (Damadio, NAMTA Journal 28/1, p. 74) — at the height of "a kind of sensitive period for the imagination" (From Childhood to Adolescence, ch. 5).
  • The full 6–12 range of materials in the room. If the school splits into 6–9 and 9–12, "each class must have a complete set of materials for the whole 6-to-12 range […] it is very common for a 7-year-old to do 11-year-old work, and vice versa" — or a door always open between the two rooms (Grazzini, qtd. in Stephenson, pp. 155–156).
  • The five areas visible and accessible. The most common defect: mathematics and language in abundance, with the timelines and science material "in cupboards, in the teacher's room, or simply unavailable" (Stephenson, p. 154).
  • Big work, not small repetition. The child of this plane "is interested in big and extraordinary things. Big mathematics problems […] interest her; small problems bore her" (Gupta, NAMTA 17/1).
  • The cost of not sowing is high: concepts not presented between 6 and 9 will be grasped only shallowly from 9 to 12, "because at that age they no longer want to spend the time needed to abstract the concepts" (Gupta, NAMTA 17/1).

The six-to-nine-year-olds, as you know, are so sweet, so open-hearted. Everything you give them is received with joy and enthusiasm.
Damadio, Suzanne A. "The Six-to-Twelve Working Model". NAMTA Journal 28/1 (p. 74).

If certain rudimentary concepts of science, mathematics, geography and other areas of the curriculum are not presented between six and nine, in the period from nine to twelve their understanding will remain shallow, because at that age they no longer want to spend the time needed to abstract the concepts.
Gupta, Rajendra. NAMTA Journal 17/1.

Upper Elementary (9–12): analysis and application

If 6–9 is sowing, 9–12 is deepening. The mentality is the same — reasoning mind, imagination, moral sense — but the direction of the work changes: "their interest is mainly in the application of the concepts" (Gupta, NAMTA 17/1); the child "wants more information and wants to go deeper" (Damadio, NAMTA 28/1, p. 74).

  • Fewer new materials, more extensions — research, projects, big self-initiated work; the environment answers with depth, not with new shelves (Damadio, NAMTA 28/1, p. 81).
  • The concrete bridge still stands. The passage to abstraction is progressive: even in early adolescence students "still benefit from 'materialized abstractions', such as area, volume and the binomial and trinomial cubes" (Ewert-Krocker, NAMTA 26/3, p. 458) — each child leaves the material at their own moment.
  • The world of work enters the range of going out. "Children between 10 and 12 should visit the places of work and production: industrial centers, factories […] to learn under what conditions man produces the different articles" (Montessori, qtd. in Baker, NAMTA 26/1, p. 79). The outings grow longer and are "entirely organized by the children themselves" (Stephenson, p. 159).
  • Growing responsibility for one's own records: work journals, plans and conferences with the guide — the environment includes the instruments of that self-management (Stephenson).
  • Studies return at a more advanced level: the same themes of the Great Lessons, revisited with instruments of analysis (Duffy & Duffy, Children of the Universe, p. 103).
  • The quality of 9–12 depends on 6–9: it "depends greatly on how the seeds of science and culture were sown in the 6-to-9 years" (Gupta, NAMTA 17/1).

Children between 10 and 12 should visit the places of work and production; that is, industrial centers, factories, see how minerals are extracted from ores, come into contact with the products of civilization, in order to learn under what conditions man produces the different articles.
Montessori, Maria — qtd. in Baker, NAMTA Journal 26/1 (p. 79).

The two subphases side by side:

Lower Elementary (6–9)Upper Elementary (9–12)
Dominant activityAccumulation of cultural raw materialAnalysis and application of what was accumulated
DispositionOpenness, wonder, joyful receptionThirst for information and depth
MaterialsComplete set, intense concrete useFewer new lessons; extensions, research, projects
AbstractionUnder construction upon the materialProgressive detachment, at each child's pace
Going outShort, near, frequentLong and complex; the world of work (10–12)
RecordsGuided by the teacherProgressively taken over by the student

The mentality is the same in both columns — which is why Grazzini requires the complete 6–12 set of materials in each room.

An environment that grows with the child

The prepared environment is never fixed. It follows the plane of development it serves — and, within the plane, the children who inhabit it today. Observation tells the guide what needs to be added, removed or adjusted. Preparing the environment is not a task done once at the start of the year; it is a daily act of respect for the child. And preparing to go out — planning, phoning, budgeting, returning — is itself an exercise: the aim "is not the outing, but to make the spiritual being it educates capable of finding the way by himself" (From Childhood to Adolescence, ch. 4).

Education is a natural process carried out by the human individual, and is acquired not by listening to words, but by experiences in the environment.
Montessori, Maria. Education for a New World (p. 3).
