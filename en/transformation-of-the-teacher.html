From instructor to guide

The Transformation of the Teacher

Montessori did not ask the teacher merely to learn new techniques. She asked for something far more demanding: a transformation of the adult — from the center of the classroom to the servant of the child's development.

The spiritual preparation

Before preparing the environment, the adult must prepare themselves. Montessori called this the spiritual preparation: a sincere and continuous study of one's self, to uncover the defects — pride, anger, the need to control — that stand between the adult and the child. It is not about becoming perfect; it is about becoming aware, so that our own obstacles do not become the child's obstacles.

We insist on the fact that a teacher must prepare himself interiorly by systematically studying himself so that he can tear out his most deeply rooted defects, those in fact which impede his relations with children. In order to discover these subconscious failings, we have need of a special kind of instruction. We must see ourselves as another sees us.
Montessori, Maria. The Secret of Childhood.

The new role in practice

The transformed adult acts differently. They prepare the environment and keep it alive; they observe before intervening; they present with precision and then withdraw; they protect concentration as the most precious event in the room. Their authority no longer comes from being the source of all knowledge, but from serving the child's own construction of knowledge.

Not words, but virtues, are her main qualifications.
Montessori, Maria. The Discovery of the Child.

This is the passage from instructor to guide: from the one who transmits to the one who prepares, observes and supports. The measure of success is inverted — the better the guide works, the less the children appear to need them.

A daily practice

The transformation is never finished. It is renewed every day, through reflective practices: reviewing our observations, questioning our reactions, preparing ourselves each morning to receive each child without the residue of yesterday's frustrations. The guide who reflects daily models, in silence, exactly what the children are building in themselves — self-awareness, self-discipline and care.

This is why the Guiding Principles ask the adult to be "a living example of normalization": we cannot invite the child to a calm, concentrated and respectful life if our own presence contradicts it.

What the transformation makes possible

When the adult steps back and the work begins, the child's true nature appears.
