From instructor to guide

The Transformation of the Teacher

Montessori education does not begin with materials or lessons. It begins with a change of state in the adult — one that cannot be bought, taught directly, or forced: giving up the belief that they are the maker of the child, and learning to serve a development that only the child can carry out.

A new kind of teacher

The Montessori teacher's starting point is not a curriculum but a change of vision. Where the traditional teacher manages the children in front of her, the Montessori guide works on behalf of a child she cannot yet see — the concentrated, self-disciplined child hidden inside the restless one.

  • Preparation is self-study first. "The real preparation for education is a study of one's self" — training of character and spirit, far more than a learning of ideas (The Absorbent Mind, p. 153).
  • Faith in the unseen child. The teacher "is constantly looking for a child who is not yet there" and "must have a kind of faith that the child will reveal himself through work" (The Absorbent Mind, ch. 27, p. 311).
  • From maker to servant. The teacher stops thinking of herself as the one who forms the child, and instead serves the child's own constructive energies — Montessori's image is the discreet valet who prepares everything and then disappears (ch. 27, pp. 315–316).
  • Disorder is feedback, not offense. When the class loses discipline, the transformed teacher looks for her own error and corrects it, rather than blaming the children (ch. 27, p. 320).
  • A lifelong process. Modern scholarship reads this preparation as "one of the most radical ideas of Montessori's work, demanding never-ending development" — a continuing formation, not a certificate earned once (Jendza, Bloomsbury Handbook of Montessori Education, p. 167).

The real preparation for education is a study of one's self. The training of the teacher who is to help life is something far more than a learning of ideas. It includes the training of character; it is a preparation of the spirit.
Montessori, Maria. The Absorbent Mind (ch. 14, p. 153).

The spiritual preparation: anger and pride

In The Secret of Childhood, Montessori is unusually blunt about what stands between adults and children. The obstacle is not a lack of method; it is a pair of moral defects that adults barely notice in themselves — because children never resist them.

  • Inner examination before outer technique. "The educator must prepare himself inwardly. He must examine himself methodically in order to discover certain definite defects that may become obstacles in his relation with the child" (The Secret of Childhood, ch. 6, p. 115).
  • The two named defects. Anger is the essential sin against the child; pride arrives to give it "a pleasing camouflage" (ch. 6, pp. 116, 118).
  • Tyranny in respectable clothes. Fused together, anger and pride take on "that precise, quiet, and respectable shape, known as tyranny" — the assumption that the adult is right simply because they are the adult (ch. 6, pp. 119–120).
  • Why children are uniquely vulnerable. Adults restrain their anger before the strong; children believe everything, forgive everything, and even feel guilty for our accusations — so they become the outlet (ch. 6, p. 119).
  • The remedy. Not perfectionism, but humility and charity — and never passivity: the aim remains "to educate, to become a real helper to the child" (ch. 6, p. 121).

[He] must tear down that ancient complex of pride and anger that unconsciously encrusts his heart; strip himself of pride and anger and become humble; this first of all; then clothe himself in charity.
Montessori, Maria. The Secret of Childhood (ch. 6, p. 121).

The three stages of the teacher's work

Chapter 27 of The Absorbent Mind gives the transformation a practical shape. The teacher's behavior is not one fixed style; it changes deliberately as the class moves toward concentration — and the turning point reverses her rules of action.

1
Keeper of the environment — materials meticulous, complete and attractive; the teacher's own appearance and movements are part of the environment (pp. 311–312).
2
The flame — before concentration exists there is nothing to interrupt, so she may charm, sing, tell stories, and even deliberately interrupt a disturbing child (pp. 312–314).
3
Non-interference — once real interest appears, praise, help, or even a look can shatter it; she protects the work and serves "the child's spirit" (pp. 314–316).

The pivot: before the first concentration, intervene freely; after it, "act as if the child does not exist" (p. 315).

The teacher's skill in not interfering comes with practice, like everything else, but it never comes very easily. It means rising to spiritual heights. True spirituality realizes that even to help can be a source of pride.
Montessori, Maria. The Absorbent Mind (ch. 27, p. 315).

A daily practice — and the measure of success

The transformation is never finished. It renews itself every day, through reflective practice: reviewing observations, questioning one's own reactions, preparing each morning to receive each child without the residue of yesterday's frustrations. That is why the Guiding Principles ask the adult to be "a living example of normalization": we cannot invite the child to a calm, concentrated, respectful life if our own presence contradicts it.

And the direction of influence, in the end, reverses: it is the normalized children who elevate the adult — "the child has made her grow till she is brought within his sphere" (The Absorbent Mind, ch. 27, p. 318).

When the children show her their real natures, she understands, perhaps for the first time, what love really is. And this revelation transforms her also.
Montessori, Maria. The Absorbent Mind (ch. 27, p. 317).

What is the greatest sign of success for a teacher thus transformed? It is to be able to say, "The children are now working as if I did not exist."
Montessori, Maria. The Absorbent Mind (ch. 27, p. 318).

Experience tells us that we must not consider the teacher and the child separately… The adult, the child, and the environment are a trinity. They have to be considered as one.
Montessori, Maria. The 1946 London Lectures (lecture 30, p. 274).
