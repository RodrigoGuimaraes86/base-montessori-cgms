Theory and practice

Child Development

Maria Montessori belongs to the generation of thinkers that made the twentieth century "the century of the child". Placing her work beside Piaget, Vygotsky and Erikson — and beside the reality of children who learn differently — sharpens our understanding of what the method is for.

Influential theorists

It is no coincidence that Montessori's ideas converse so closely with those of the great developmental theorists: Erik Erikson and Anna Freud went through Montessori's training courses in Vienna, and Piaget directed a "modified" Montessori school and presided over the Swiss Montessori Society before going his own way (Haines, NAMTA Journal 25/1, 2000). Understanding these theorists helps the Montessorian in two directions: to see what later research confirmed in Montessori's observations, and to explain the method in a language that educators, families and psychologists outside the movement recognize.

The child is the builder of man… It is the child who makes the man, and no man exists who was not made by the child he once was.
Montessori, Maria. The Absorbent Mind.

Jean Piaget (1896–1980)

Swiss psychologist, creator of genetic epistemology — the study of how knowledge is constructed in the child.

  • The child constructs their own knowledge. Knowledge is not received ready-made from the adult: it is built through the child's action on the world — the central thesis of constructivism.
  • Assimilation, accommodation and equilibration. The child incorporates new experiences into existing mental schemas; when the experience does not fit, the schema reorganizes itself; the engine of development is the search for equilibrium between the two.
  • Qualitatively distinct stages, always in the same order: sensorimotor (0–2), preoperational (2–7), concrete operational (7–11, when conservation, classification, seriation and reversibility appear — still anchored in the concrete) and formal operational (from ~11–12, hypothetico-deductive reasoning).
  • Error is informative: it reveals the schema the child is thinking with, and cognitive conflict (disequilibrium) is an occasion for learning.
  • Peer interaction matters, because children at close levels of understanding provoke productive disequilibria in one another (Lillard, ch. 7).

Lev Vygotsky (1896–1934)

Belarusian-Soviet psychologist, founder of the sociocultural perspective on development.

  • Development is social before it is individual. The higher psychological functions appear first between people, in interaction, and only later within the child, internalized.
  • Zone of proximal development (ZPD): the distance between what the child does alone and what they can do with the help of someone more experienced. It is in the ZPD that teaching is fertile; short of it, teaching is redundant; far beyond it, sterile.
  • Mediation by tools and signs. Between the child and the world stand cultural tools — language first of all — that structure perception and thought.
  • Language organizes thought: the young child's egocentric speech is not a defect — it is thinking aloud on its way to becoming inner speech.
  • Scaffolding: the temporary, adjusted support the more experienced partner offers and withdraws as the child becomes capable (a term coined later, by Wood, Bruner and Ross, building on Vygotsky).
  • Vygotsky knew and praised Montessori — he recognized in her the appreciation of the naturalness of early literacy, in line with his thesis that writing has a "pre-history" in gesture and play (Loeffler, NAMTA Journal 18/2, 1993).

Erik Erikson (1902–1994)

German-American psychoanalyst — who trained as a Montessori teacher in Vienna before emigrating (Haines, NAMTA Journal 25/1, 2000). He broadened Freudian theory into a psychosocial theory spanning the whole of life.

  • Eight psychosocial stages, each defined by a crisis — a tension between a favorable and an unfavorable pole — whose resolution leaves a strength ("virtue") or a fragility in the personality: trust × mistrust (0–1½), autonomy × shame (1½–3), initiative × guilt (3–6), industry × inferiority (6–12), identity × role confusion (adolescence), and the three crises of adult life.
  • The elementary child's stage is industry: the child wants to make real things, make them well, and be recognized for it; the risk is a sense of inferiority when the effort fails or is devalued.
  • Development is psychosocial, not merely maturational: each crisis plays out in relation to a widening social radius — mother, family, school, peer group, society.
  • Basic trust, built in the first stage through predictability and responsive care, is the foundation of everything that follows — a point where Erikson echoes the Montessori emphasis on the infant's order and routine (Helfrich, ch. 9).

Montessori's own map: the four planes

Against these maps, Montessori lays down her own stage theory: development is not the gradual accretion of a being always identical to itself, but a succession of distinct psychic types — four six-year planes, alternating creative phases of great transformation (1st and 3rd) and stable phases of crystallization (2nd and 4th) (Helfrich, ch. 3). The elementary child lives the three metamorphoses of the second plane: the need to widen the field of action, the passage to the abstract plane and the awakening of the moral sense — it is the "sensitive period of culture", when "everything must be sown" (From Childhood to Adolescence, ch. 2 and Conclusion).

Development is a series of rebirths.
Montessori, Maria. The Absorbent Mind (ch. 3, p. 30).

The next period goes from six to twelve years. It is a period of growth unaccompanied by other changes. The child is calm and happy. Mentally, he is in a state of health, strength and assured stability.
Montessori, Maria. The Absorbent Mind (ch. 3, p. 31).

Successive levels of education must correspond to the successive personalities of the child. Our methods are oriented not to any pre-established principles, but to the inherent characteristics of the different ages.
Montessori, Maria. From Childhood to Adolescence (ch. 1).

The four planes and the parallel stages — compare the thresholds: Piaget's turn to concrete operations at ~7, Erikson's industry crisis at 6–12 and Montessori's second plane all point to the same child.

The planes have a whole page of their own; here, what matters is to see them as a stage theory standing on equal footing with the others. It is the "sensitive period of culture", "during which the abstract plane of the human mind is organized […] It is then that everything must be sown" (From Childhood to Adolescence, Conclusion).

Education should not limit itself to seeking new methods for a mostly arid transmission of knowledge: its aim must be to give the necessary aid to human development.
Montessori, Maria. From Childhood to Adolescence (Conclusion).

Comparisons and contrasts

Montessori and Piaget

Convergences

  • Both are constructivists before the name: the child is the agent of their own formation, not a recipient (Haines, 2000).
  • Qualitative stages with nearly identical thresholds: concrete operations (7–11) coincide with the second plane (6–12).
  • Error as part of learning — in Piaget, disequilibrium; in Montessori, the control of error in the material, which makes the adult's correction unnecessary.

Contrasts

  • Senses and hand: for Montessori, intelligence is built through movement and the work of the hand; Piaget shifts the interest to internal logical structures.
  • Readiness × sensitive period: the Piagetian stage tends to be read as a limit ("not yet able"); the sensitive period is a window of opportunity ("now is the time") — and research has sided with Montessori where Piaget underestimated the young child (Lillard, preface).
  • Method: Piaget observed in order to describe; Montessori observed in order to intervene in the environment — hers is a theory born as pedagogy.
  • After presiding over the Swiss Montessori Society, Piaget dismissed the method in 1965 — while the Montessori vocabulary ("hands-on", manipulatives, intrinsic motivation) kept circulating uncredited (Haines, 2000).

Montessori and Vygotsky

Convergences

  • The ZPD meets the material: the control of error and the points of interest operate as mediating "signs" that carry the child beyond what they would do alone (Haines, 2000).
  • The three-age grouping is an institutionalized ZPD: the one who watches learns, the one who teaches consolidates (Lillard, ch. 7).
  • Both take language and culture seriously; Vygotsky praised Montessori's natural approach to early literacy by name (Loeffler, NAMTA 18/2, 1993).

Contrasts

  • Who mediates? In Vygotsky, the more experienced person; in Montessori, the adult deliberately withdraws and transfers mediation to the prepared environment — the scaffold is the material, not the teacher's speech.
  • Individual concentration × joint activity: the typical Vygotskian scene is dialogue; the founding Montessori scene is the child absorbed, alone, in self-chosen work.
  • Symbolic play: Vygotsky sees make-believe as the leading activity of the preschool age; Montessori preferred to offer the young child real participation in life — fantasy detached from the real seemed to her escape, not nourishment (Lillard, ch. 6).

Montessori and Erikson

Convergences

  • The filiation is direct: Erikson trained as a Montessori teacher in Vienna (Haines, 2000).
  • The industry crisis (6–12) is the psychosocial portrait of the second plane: real work, big projects, recognized competence — exactly what the great lessons, group work and going out provide.
  • Basic trust echoes the sensitive period for order: predictable routine and responsive care (Helfrich, ch. 9).
  • Both extend development across the whole of life, as the subject's own task.

Contrasts

  • Affective axis × activity axis: Erikson tells us what is at stake emotionally at each age; Montessori tells us what to do in the environment. More complementary than rival.
  • The outcome of the crisis: in Erikson, it depends largely on how adults and peers respond to the child's effort; Montessori moves the judgment inward — no grades, no comparison — so that competence is measured by the work itself.
  • Evidence base: Erikson starts from the psychoanalytic clinic; Montessori, from the systematic observation of children in free activity — "scientific pedagogy".

Practical application to our work

What these theories, read together, change in the practice of an elementary classroom:

  • To follow the child is to follow the plane. Plan from the characteristics of the age — reasoning mind, imagination, hunger for justice, gregarious instinct — not from an abstract syllabus (From Childhood to Adolescence, ch. 1).
  • From concrete to abstract, material in hand (Piaget + Montessori): the concrete-operational child reasons about what they manipulate; mathematics begins with the golden beads and "rises" to abstraction at each child's pace.
  • Multi-age grouping as a permanent ZPD (Vygotsky): protect and cultivate spontaneous peer tutoring (Lillard, ch. 7).
  • Brief lesson, minimal help (Vygotsky + Montessori): present in the proximal zone — neither the already mastered nor the unreachable — and remove the scaffold: the material stays, the adult leaves.
  • Real work that builds competence (Erikson): long projects, authentic responsibilities, outings planned by the child — that is how the industry crisis resolves on the side of competence.
  • No grades, no stars (Erikson + Piaget): assessment through the control of error, observation and self-assessment; error treated as information, never as shame (Lillard, ch. 6).
  • Feed the imagination with the cosmos (Montessori): in the sensitive period of culture, "sow the seeds of all the sciences" through the great lessons (From Childhood to Adolescence, ch. 5).
  • Moral education through social life, not sermons: justice, rules and commitment discussed and lived in the classroom community.
  • Observe before intervening: in all three theories, the effective adult is the one who sees where the child stands in their development. Montessori observation is the daily instrument for that.

My vision of the future is no longer of people taking exams… but of individuals passing from one stage of independence to a higher, by means of their own activity, through their own effort of will.
Montessori, Maria. From Childhood to Adolescence (epigraph).

Learning exceptionalities

Worth remembering from the start: the Montessori method was born in special education. It was in the work with the children deemed "deficient" at the Rome asylum, and in the study of Itard and Séguin, that Montessori forged her materials — and the children of the Orthophrenic School passed the state examinations alongside the others (Helfrich, ch. 1). Attention to the child who learns differently is therefore foundational — and the NAMTA Journal devoted entire issues to the theme (33/2, 34/2, 39/3, 42/2).

Overview in the elementary years (6–12)

"Exceptionality" covers both the child with a condition that hinders school learning and the child with abilities far above average. In broad lines, following the categories of the American IDEA law:

  • Specific learning disorders — the most frequent category in the elementary years: dyslexia (decoding and reading fluency, phonological in origin — it responds to structured multisensory instruction, which the Orton-Gillingham tradition developed in remarkable parallel with Montessori (NAMTA 34/2; Awes, NAMTA 39/3)), dysgraphia (handwriting and spelling) and dyscalculia (number, quantity and calculation).
  • ADHD — inattention, impulsivity and/or hyperactivity to a degree that interferes with functioning; in Montessori it calls for management of the environment, protected work cycles and, often, partnership with an occupational therapist and physician (NAMTA 42/2).
  • Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) — differences in social communication and restricted/repetitive patterns, across a wide spectrum; the predictability, order and concrete material of the Montessori room are allies, with individual adaptations (NAMTA 33/2; 34/2).
  • Speech and language disorders — from articulation errors to developmental language disorder; they frequently coexist with literacy difficulties (NAMTA 39/3).
  • Sensory processing disorder — atypical (hyper or hypo) responses to sensory input, affecting regulation and participation; a classic area of collaboration with occupational therapy (NAMTA 34/2; 39/3).
  • Emotional and behavioral disorders — anxiety, depression, dysregulation, challenging behaviors, including children in crisis or trauma (NAMTA 42/2).
  • Intellectual disability — significant limitations in intellectual and adaptive functioning, originating in the developmental period.
  • Sensory and physical disabilities — low vision/blindness, hearing loss/deafness, motor limitations and chronic health conditions.
  • Giftedness — the "upward" exceptionality: it calls for depth and acceleration, which the multi-age room accommodates naturally.
  • Twice-exceptionality (2e) — the child simultaneously gifted and with a disorder, each condition masking the other and delaying identification.

Points of attention for the guide

  • Observation comes first: the Montessori instruments (engagement, concentration, choice of work) are the first screen of identification — and what is observed guides the referral for professional evaluation (Leigh-Doyle; Hughes, NAMTA 34/2).
  • The classroom does not replace diagnosis or specialized intervention: the path is referral → evaluation → intervention, in partnership with neuropsychologists, speech-language pathologists and occupational therapists.
  • Inclusion is the reorganization of the whole school, not the kindness of one classroom: staff training, communication with the family, and an individual plan (NAMTA 39/3).

How the Montessori environment helps

  • Multisensory materials engage sight, touch and movement — a support for many learners.
  • Control of error lets the child correct themselves, protecting confidence.
  • Individual pace and long work cycles remove time pressure.
  • Freedom of movement serves children who need to move to focus.
  • Mixed-age community allows a child to work at their level without stigma.
  • Concrete-to-abstract sequence gives every learner a path into abstraction.

Glossary

Accommodation
A change in how the child learns or shows what they know (more time, enlarged text, reading support), without altering the expected content.
Modification
A change in what the child is expected to learn — the content or criterion itself is altered.
Differentiation
Adjusting teaching to the varied readiness, interests and needs of learners.
Inclusion
Organizing the school so that the child with a disability learns in the regular classroom with the necessary supports — a reorganization of the whole, not mere physical placement.
IDEA
Individuals with Disabilities Education Act: the American federal law guaranteeing a free appropriate public education to children with disabilities, in 13 categories.
LRE (Least Restrictive Environment)
The legal principle (IDEA) that the child should be educated, as far as possible, alongside their peers.
IEP
Individualized Education Program: the legally binding individual plan — goals, services, accommodations — for the student eligible under IDEA.
504 Plan
An accommodations plan (US civil-rights law) for a student with a condition limiting a major life activity, without requiring IDEA eligibility.
RTI / MTSS
Response to Intervention / Multi-Tiered System of Supports: a tiered model — quality teaching for all, small-group intervention, intensive individual intervention.
Dyslexia
A specific, neurobiological learning disorder affecting reading accuracy and fluency and spelling, typically through a phonological deficit.
Dysgraphia
A specific disorder affecting handwriting, spelling and the organization of written text.
Dyscalculia
A specific disorder affecting number sense, calculation and mathematical reasoning.
Phonemic awareness
The ability to perceive and manipulate the individual sounds of speech; a prerequisite of alphabetic writing and a typical weak point in dyslexia.
Orton-Gillingham
A multisensory, structured, sequential approach to teaching reading to dyslexic learners; historically parallel to the Montessori method (NAMTA 34/2).
ADHD
Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder: a persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that interferes with functioning.
ASD (Autism Spectrum Disorder)
A neurodevelopmental condition marked by differences in social communication and by restricted/repetitive interests and behaviors, on a spectrum.
Developmental language disorder
Persistent difficulty in acquiring and using language (comprehension and/or expression) not explained by another condition.
Sensory processing disorder
Difficulty modulating and organizing responses to sensory input (hyper/hypo-reactivity), affecting regulation and participation.
Executive function
The self-regulation processes — working memory, inhibitory control, flexibility, planning — frequently affected in ADHD.
Occupational therapy (OT)
The health profession that supports participation in life's occupations; at school it works on sensory regulation, fine-motor coordination and executive function.
Giftedness
Performance or potential markedly above average in an intellectual, academic, artistic or leadership area.
Twice-exceptional (2e)
The coexistence of giftedness and a disorder (learning, attention, ASD), in which one condition can mask the other.
Scaffolding
Temporary, adjusted support given by the more experienced partner, withdrawn as the child gains autonomy (a concept derived from Vygotsky).
ZPD (Zone of Proximal Development)
In Vygotsky, the distance between what the child does alone and what they do with help; the region where teaching is fertile.
