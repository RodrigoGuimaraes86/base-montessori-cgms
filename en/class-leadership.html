Leading the community

Class Leadership

In the traditional school, "leading the class" usually means controlling it. In the Montessori perspective the meaning is different: the guide leads the way a scientist leads a laboratory and a host leads a home — preparing the environment, observing each child, protecting everyone's work, and stepping back whenever the child can act alone.

The adult's authority does not disappear: it shifts. Instead of holding order in place with rewards and punishments, the guide builds the conditions for order to be born from within — from concentration, belonging and real responsibility. The six dimensions on this page walk through the tools of that leadership: planning and record keeping, grace and courtesy, community building, informed guidance and positive discipline, rights and responsibilities, and the partnership with families.

Inner discipline is something to come, and not something already present. Our task is to show the way to discipline.
Montessori, Maria. The Absorbent Mind (ch. 26, p. 297).

Planning and record keeping

The Montessori method was born from observation — and Montessori planning remains, above all, recorded observation. The guide does not plan "the class lesson": they plan the next step for each child, and can only do so because they note, day after day, what each one chose, repeated, mastered or avoided. The record is not bureaucracy: it is the scientific instrument that replaces the test and the grade — and it is what makes freedom possible, because the child can choose freely without ever being lost.

  • Observe before planning. The plan grows out of what the child has shown, not out of a fixed timetable. Observation is a professional discipline to be trained, not improvised (NAMTA Journal 41/3, 2016).
  • An individual weekly plan. At the start of the week the guide selects about three possible lessons for each child, on a clipboard kept at hand all day; lessons given are circled, mastery is marked, corrections are noted for "a neutral moment"; at the end of the week the data generate the next week's plan (Stephenson, Please Help Me Do It Myself, pp. 44–49).
  • Mastery charts. Maps per area — practical life, sensorial, cultural subjects — show at a glance where each child is and what may come next (Stephenson, pp. 50–85).
  • The teacher's journal. It keeps what numbers cannot: scenes, hypotheses, worries — the raw material of reflection (Stephenson, pp. 101–115).
  • Record yourself as well. The guide notes their own interventions and reviews them: the first question before a class that unravels is "what am I doing?".

I observed for myself the exuberance and generosity with which the child's nature responds to scientific education. That observation left me thoughtful and full of wonder; and I became a faithful follower of the spirit of the child.
Montessori, Maria. Citizen of the World (p. 97).

Grace and courtesy

Grace and courtesy lessons are the Montessori answer to a question every school faces: how do you teach someone to live with others? The answer is neither the sermon nor the poster of rules — it is the short, joyful, rehearsed lesson that gives the child the movements and the words of each social situation: greeting, thanking, interrupting without trampling, offering help, declining gently. Taught in a neutral moment (never as a correction in the heat of the mistake), the lesson becomes repertoire; in time, repertoire becomes character.

  • Grace and courtesy are two things. Grace looks inward: it is the perception of one's own dignity. Courtesy moves outward: it is respect for the being of the other. Civility holds the two together in daily life (Ludick, NAMTA Journal 40/1, 2015, pp. 9–30).
  • The essence is presence. Good manners are the visible face of an inner process; the heart of courtesy is being fully present to the other (Schaefer, NAMTA Journal 40/1, 2015, pp. 31–49).
  • Taught by the lesson, consolidated by life. The lesson gives the gesture and the vocabulary; social virtue only develops through real, frequent experience in the community (Black, NAMTA Journal 39/1, 2014, pp. 9–20).
  • Not memorized etiquette: discernment. The goal is a child able to choose, alone, the conduct each moment calls for.
  • It applies to the adult first. The most effective grace and courtesy lesson is the way the guide speaks with the children and with the other adults of the school.

The child has learned not only to be silent, but when he ought to be silent. He has learned not only the various forms of greeting, but which to use with another child, with his mother or father, with a stranger, or with someone old and respected. In other words, he must use, according to the moment and the circumstances, the many things he has learned to perfection.
Montessori, Maria. The Discovery of the Child (p. 121).

Community building

A Montessori class is not a collection of same-age students covering the same syllabus: it is a small three-age society, which Montessori called a society by cohesion — united not by obedience to a chief, but by the spontaneous interweaving of work, help and affection (The Absorbent Mind, chs. 22–23). Class leadership consists of preparing the conditions of that cohesion with the same intentionality devoted to the shelves and the materials.

  • Mixed ages on purpose. The younger learns by watching the older; the older consolidates what they know by guiding the younger. Independence matures into interdependence (Black, NAMTA Journal 39/1, 2014).
  • One of each material. The limitation is pedagogical: waiting one's turn exercises, every day, patience, will and respect for the work of others.
  • Emotional safety. Community is where one can err without fear of ridicule — and where help is offered only when clearly needed, in exact measure, leaving dignity intact.
  • Care of the environment as a communal response. When the community works, children notice on their own the plant to water and the shelf to tidy — and the chore chart becomes unnecessary.
  • Ritual and celebration. The school's own ceremonies — the opening of the year, the passages between environments — sustain the common culture and give the sense of belonging to something larger (Schaefer, NAMTA Journal 40/1, 2015).
  • The evidence agrees. Contemporary research on peer learning confirms what the multi-age class has practiced for over a century (Lillard, The Science Behind the Genius, ch. 7).

Order and discipline must be aimed at the attainment of human harmony, and any act that hinders the establishment of a genuine community of all mankind must be regarded as immoral and a threat to the life of society.
Montessori, Maria. Education and Peace (p. 13).

Informed guidance and positive discipline

For Montessori, discipline is not imposed: it is built. It is born when the child concentrates on work they have chosen and that interests them — and the adult's role is to distinguish, as the physician distinguishes the physiological from the pathological, ordered spontaneous energy from disordered impulse: respecting the first, interrupting the second with serene firmness (The Absorbent Mind, ch. 26). Positive Discipline, Adlerian in origin, offers the contemporary guide a toolkit convergent with this vision: kind and firm at the same time, neither punitive nor permissive.

  • Freedom is not abandonment. Montessori freedom is freedom within limits, and it grows together with the child's capacity for self-control.
  • Obedience matures in three levels — from obeying only when the request coincides with one's own impulse, to being able to obey always, to obeying with joy — because the will must not be broken, but cultivated (The Absorbent Mind, ch. 25).
  • Misbehavior is discouragement. In the Adlerian reading, the misbehaving child is a discouraged child seeking belonging through a mistaken goal; the effective response begins with connection, before correction (Nelsen & DeLorenzo, Positive Discipline in the Montessori Classroom, chs. 2–4).
  • Solutions, not punishments. Instead of imposed consequences, the joint search for solutions — including class meetings, where the community itself examines its problems and decides how to solve them (Nelsen & DeLorenzo, chs. 8, 15–17).
  • Be informed by the data too. Informed guidance includes examining one's own practice: research in American public schools showed that even Montessori schools suspend Black students disproportionately — watching one's own bias is part of class leadership (Brown & Steele, Journal of Montessori Research, 1/1, 2015).

Discipline must come through liberty. […] We do not consider an individual disciplined only when he has been rendered as artificially silent as a mute and as immovable as a paralytic. He is an individual annihilated, not disciplined.
Montessori, Maria. The Montessori Method (p. 86).

To let the child do as he likes when he has not yet developed any powers of control is to betray the idea of freedom.
Montessori, Maria. The Absorbent Mind (p. 232).

Rights and responsibilities

Montessori was an advocate for children's rights before the phrase existed in the treaties: in a message to UNESCO in 1951 she denounced that the child — "father of the man", producer of adult humanity — had been left out of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, reduced to an object of assistance, and claimed the child's fundamental right to be helped in their constructive energies ("The Forgotten Citizen", in Citizen of the World, pp. 64–72). In the classroom, that conviction becomes a daily pact: each freedom corresponds to a responsibility toward the community — and the guide is the guardian of that balance.

  • The child's rights in the class: to choose their own work; to work without being interrupted; to err and correct themselves through the material's control of error; not to be helped unnecessarily; to be treated with the same respect owed to an adult.
  • The corresponding responsibilities: to return the material complete to its place; to respect the work and concentration of others; to care for the common environment; to use with their companions the courtesy they receive.
  • Freedom grows with capacity. The "steps to freedom" are earned one by one: new licenses as the child demonstrates new controls — and that progress is part of the guide's records (Stephenson, pp. 56–59).
  • Responsibility springs from belonging, not from imposed rosters: where the community works, children take up the care of the environment without a chore chart.
  • The horizon is civic. The child who exercises real rights and responsibilities in the small society of the class is being prepared to exercise them in the larger one — education as social reconstruction, which Montessori preached all her life.

The child must acquire physical independence by being sufficient unto himself; he must become of independent will by using in freedom his own power of choice; he must become capable of independent thought by working alone without interruption. […] We have to help the child to act, will and think for himself. This is the art of serving the spirit.
Montessori, Maria. The Absorbent Mind (p. 316).

Parent communication and education

No class is led only from the inside. The child lives in a system — home and school — and research shows that this system talks less than it assumes: in a study with Montessori teachers and families, 53% of teachers bet that families' number-one priority would be academic progress; families put it in fifth place, and chose "learning to be kind" and "learning to be respectful" as the top two (Epstein, Journal of Montessori Research, 1/1, 2015). Communicating with parents therefore begins like all Montessori leadership: observing and asking before prescribing.

  • From the linear model to the partnership model. Each party contributes its own expertise: Montessori gives us the universal child, parents know the individual child, the guide knows the social child — all three perspectives are true and add up (Werner Andrews, NAMTA Journal 40/1, 2015, pp. 129–137).
  • Ask priorities, don't presume them. Learn what each family values and what weighs on it — ideally before a problem appears.
  • Patterns, not labels. In difficult conversations, describe observed interactions ("when X happens, she responds Y") instead of labeling the child or the parents; and "water what you want to grow": look for the circumstances in which things go well and multiply them.
  • Communication that replaces the grade: individual conferences, narrative reports built from observation, and portfolios of the child's real work.
  • Parent education as a program, not a note home. Themed meetings, observation mornings in the classroom, evenings where the adults handle the materials, a lending library for families: parents can only sustain at home what they understand — and Montessori wrote directly for them.
  • The same respect as in the classroom, at the front desk. Grace and courtesy with families: the school that asks presence and listening of its children must offer them to the parents.

The spontaneous urge towards development which is within the child dictates its own pace. It is for the wise and loving parent to stand by, to follow the little one's activities, and to observe his growth — rather than to try to force it.
Montessori, Maria. Maria Montessori Speaks to Parents (p. 35).

We may love our children so much that we are blinded to what is best for them. We may wish so ardently for them to become good men and women that we check and thwart them at every turn, without realizing that they have within themselves the power of their own development.
Montessori, Maria. Maria Montessori Speaks to Parents (p. 34).
