Key Concepts

The Montessori Method rests on a few central ideas, scientifically observed by Maria Montessori. Understanding them is the key to grasping why the environment and the practice are the way they are.

The absorbent mind

In the first six years of life, the child learns in a way that is qualitatively different from the adult: they absorb the environment naturally, wholly and without apparent effort — the mother tongue, gestures, culture, habits. Montessori called this the absorbent mind. This is why the quality of the environment during these years has such a deep and lasting impact.

Sensitive periods

These are windows of time in which the child shows an intense, spontaneous interest in acquiring a specific ability — language, movement, order, refinement of the senses, small objects, social life. During a sensitive period, learning that ability happens with ease and joy. The adult's role is to observe and to offer, at the right moment, the appropriate opportunities.

The prepared environment

In the Montessori approach, the environment is carefully organized to foster independence. Child-sized furniture, materials within reach, order, beauty and simplicity allow the child to act on their own, choose their activities and develop concentration. Every object has its place and its purpose.

“The first task of education is to stir up life, but leave it free to develop.”

Self-education and independence

The child is the builder of themselves. Given the right environment and opportunities, they learn through their own activity — repeating, experimenting and correcting themselves. Education ceases to be something transmitted and becomes something the child accomplishes. Hence the principle: “help me to do it myself.”

The prepared adult

The Montessori teacher — often called a guide — is not the center of the room. Their role is to prepare the environment, observe each child attentively, present the materials at the right moment, and then step back to allow the child to work. This calls for preparation that is not only technical but also inner: patience, humility and trust in the child.

Freedom with responsibility

Montessori freedom is not the absence of limits — it is freedom within a clear structure. The child chooses their activity, works at their own pace and moves freely, as long as they respect the environment, the materials and their peers. It is in this balance between freedom and limits that self-discipline is born.

Normalization and concentration

Montessori observed that when a child finds an activity matching an inner need and can repeat it freely, they reach a state of deep concentration. From this concentrated work emerges what she called normalization: a calm, autonomous, sociable and contented child. It is not imposed — it is the natural flourishing that appears when conditions are right.

Control of error

The materials are designed so that the child themselves perceives and corrects their mistakes, without depending on the adult's correction. This control of error preserves autonomy, encourages persistence, and turns error into a natural part of learning.

Mixed-age groups

Classes bring together children of different ages (usually in three-year cycles). The younger children learn by observing the older ones; the older children consolidate what they know by helping the younger ones. A community of collaboration — rather than competition — is created.
